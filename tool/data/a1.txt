Aye-aye|Daubentonia madagascariensis|Aye-aye|Rainforests of Madagascar|It taps on tree bark with an extra-long, skinny middle finger and listens for hollow tunnels made by grubs.;;Once it finds a grub, it gnaws a hole with its ever-growing rodent-like incisors and hooks the larva out with that same finger.;;It is the world's largest nocturnal primate.;;In some Malagasy folklore it is considered an omen of bad luck, which has led to it being killed on sight in places.
Saiga antelope|Saiga tatarica|Saiga antelope|Steppes of Kazakhstan, Russia, Mongolia and Uzbekistan|Its huge, floppy, trunk-like nose helps filter dust in summer and warm frigid air in winter.;;In 2015 more than 200,000 saigas died within weeks from a bacterial infection linked to unusually warm, humid weather.;;Thanks to conservation efforts, numbers in Kazakhstan rebounded dramatically and the species was downlisted from Critically Endangered in 2023.;;Males' translucent, amber-coloured horns have made them a target for poaching for traditional medicine.
Okapi|Okapia johnstoni|Okapi|Rainforests of the Democratic Republic of the Congo|Despite its zebra-striped legs, its closest living relative is the giraffe.;;Its tongue is long enough to clean its own eyelids and ears.;;It was unknown to Western science until the early 1900s, earning it the nickname 'African unicorn'.;;Okapis communicate partly with infrasonic calls below the range of human hearing.
Blobfish|Psychrolutes marcidus|Psychrolutes marcidus|Deep waters off southeastern Australia and New Zealand|In its deep-sea home it looks like a fairly normal fish; the famous 'blob' look comes from decompression when it is hauled to the surface.;;Its body is mostly gelatinous flesh slightly less dense than water, letting it hover just above the sea floor without spending energy swimming.;;It lives at depths of roughly 600 to 1,200 metres.;;It was voted the world's ugliest animal in a 2013 online poll by the Ugly Animal Preservation Society.
Pink fairy armadillo|Chlamyphorus truncatus|Pink fairy armadillo|Sandy plains and grasslands of central Argentina|At around 10 cm long, it is the smallest armadillo in the world.;;Its pinkish shell is only attached to its body along a thin strip down the back.;;It spends nearly its entire life underground, 'swimming' through sand with large front claws.;;The flat, vertical plate at its rear may be used to pack down soil behind it as it digs.
Blue dragon sea slug|Glaucus atlanticus|Glaucus atlanticus|Floating on the surface of temperate and tropical oceans worldwide|It floats upside down on the ocean surface, held up by a swallowed air bubble in its stomach.;;It eats venomous Portuguese man o' war and stores their stinging cells in its own fingerlike appendages for defense.;;Because it concentrates the stingers, its sting can be more painful than the man o' war's.;;Its blue-and-silver countershading camouflages it from predators both above and below.
Shoebill|Balaeniceps rex|Shoebill|Swamps of tropical east-central Africa|Its enormous shoe-shaped bill can be over 20 cm long and ends in a sharp hook.;;It is famous for standing motionless for long periods before lunging at prey such as lungfish.;;It cools its eggs in hot weather by scooping up water in its bill and pouring it over the nest.;;Shoebills often clatter their bills together loudly as a form of communication.
Axolotl|Ambystoma mexicanum|Axolotl|Lake Xochimilco near Mexico City|It stays in its aquatic larval form its whole life, a trait called neoteny.;;It can regrow entire limbs, parts of its spinal cord, heart and even parts of its brain.;;In the wild it is critically endangered and survives almost only in the canals of Xochimilco.;;Its feathery external gills give it a permanent 'smiling with a crown' look.
Star-nosed mole|Condylura cristata|Star-nosed mole|Wetlands of eastern Canada and the northeastern United States|Its nose is ringed by 22 fleshy tentacles packed with over 25,000 touch receptors called Eimer's organs.;;It can identify and eat a food item in as little as about a quarter of a second, among the fastest foragers known.;;It can smell underwater by blowing out air bubbles and re-inhaling them.;;It is a strong swimmer and often forages in streams and ponds.
Fossa|Cryptoprocta ferox|Fossa (animal)|Forests of Madagascar|It looks like a small cougar but is actually most closely related to mongooses.;;It is the largest native carnivore on Madagascar.;;Lemurs make up a large part of its diet, and it is an agile climber that can descend trees headfirst.;;Its flexible ankles let it move through trees almost like a squirrel.
Gerenuk|Litocranius walleri|Gerenuk|Dry scrublands of the Horn of Africa and East Africa|It stands upright on its hind legs to browse leaves that other antelopes can't reach.;;Its name comes from a Somali word meaning 'giraffe-necked'.;;It can survive without drinking water, getting moisture from the plants it eats.;;Only the males have horns.
Markhor|Capra falconeri|Markhor|Mountains of Pakistan, Afghanistan and Central Asia|Males grow dramatic corkscrew-shaped horns that can reach over 1.5 metres long.;;It is the national animal of Pakistan.;;Its name is often translated from Persian as 'snake-eater', though it is a plant-eating goat.;;It climbs steep cliffs and even trees to reach food.
Dumbo octopus|Grimpoteuthis|Grimpoteuthis|Deep oceans worldwide|It is named for its ear-like fins, which it flaps to 'fly' through the water.;;Members of this group live deeper than almost any other octopus, recorded at around 7,000 metres.;;Unlike most octopuses, it has no ink sac, since there is little need for ink in the dark deep sea.;;It swallows its prey whole rather than tearing it apart.
Yeti crab|Kiwa hirsuta|Kiwa hirsuta|Hydrothermal vents in the South Pacific|It was discovered only in 2005 near Easter Island.;;Its arms are covered in hair-like setae that host colonies of bacteria.;;Scientists think it may 'farm' these bacteria and eat them.;;It is essentially blind, with only vestigial eyes.
Thorny devil|Moloch horridus|Thorny devil|Deserts of central and western Australia|Grooves between its spines draw water toward its mouth by capillary action, letting it 'drink' with its skin from damp sand.;;It eats almost nothing but ants, sometimes thousands in one meal.;;A spiny 'false head' on the back of its neck may fool predators when it tucks its real head down.;;It can change colour from pale yellows to dark browns depending on temperature.
Tardigrade|Tardigrada|Tardigrade|Almost everywhere: moss, soil, oceans and mountains worldwide|These microscopic animals, often called water bears, are usually under 1 mm long.;;In a dried-out 'tun' state they can survive extreme cold, radiation and even the vacuum of space.;;In 2007 tardigrades survived exposure to open space on the FOTON-M3 mission.;;They have eight stubby legs, each tipped with claws.
Narwhal|Monodon monoceros|Narwhal|Arctic waters around Greenland, Canada and Russia|Its 'tusk' is actually a tooth that grows through its upper lip, sometimes reaching 3 metres.;;The tusk contains millions of nerve endings and may sense changes in water.;;Narwhals can dive more than 1,500 metres deep.;;Medieval traders sold narwhal tusks as unicorn horns.
Proboscis monkey|Nasalis larvatus|Proboscis monkey|Mangrove and riverine forests of Borneo|Males have huge, pendulous noses that may amplify their calls and attract females.;;It is an excellent swimmer, with partially webbed feet.;;It has a complex, multi-chambered stomach to digest leaves, giving it a pot-bellied look.;;It is found only on the island of Borneo.
Babirusa|Babyrousa|Babirusa|Rainforests of Sulawesi and nearby Indonesian islands|Males' upper tusks grow up through the top of the snout and curve back toward the forehead.;;Its name means 'pig-deer' in Malay.;;If the tusks aren't worn down, they can keep curving and in rare cases may even pierce the skull.;;It is often seen wallowing in mud, but unlike many pigs it rarely roots in the ground with its snout.
Sunda colugo|Galeopterus variegatus|Sunda flying lemur|Forests of Southeast Asia|Despite the name 'flying lemur', it is neither a lemur nor can it truly fly; it glides.;;A membrane stretches from its neck to the tips of its fingers, toes and tail.;;It can glide over 100 metres between trees.;;Colugos are among the closest living relatives of primates.
Matamata|Chelus fimbriata|Mata mata|Slow-moving waters of the Amazon and Orinoco basins|Its flattened, leaf-like head and rough shell camouflage it as a pile of debris.;;It feeds by suddenly opening its huge mouth to create a vacuum that sucks in fish.;;It breathes through a long, snorkel-like snout while staying hidden underwater.;;It has very poor eyesight and senses prey with sensitive skin flaps on its neck.
Gharial|Gavialis gangeticus|Gharial|Rivers of northern India and Nepal|Its extremely long, thin snout is lined with over 100 sharp interlocking teeth, ideal for catching fish.;;Adult males grow a bulbous knob on the snout tip called a 'ghara', after an Indian pot.;;The ghara lets males make a buzzing sound and blow bubbles to attract females.;;It is critically endangered, with only a few hundred breeding adults left in the wild.
Kakapo|Strigops habroptilus|Kākāpō|New Zealand's predator-free offshore islands|It is the world's only flightless parrot and the heaviest parrot.;;It is nocturnal and has a strong musty, sweet smell.;;Males attract mates with deep 'booming' calls that can travel several kilometres.;;Every surviving kakapo is individually named and tracked; the population is only around 250 birds.
Mantis shrimp|Odontodactylus scyllarus|Odontodactylus scyllarus|Indo-Pacific coral reefs|Its club-like claws strike so fast that they create collapsing cavitation bubbles, hitting prey with a second shockwave.;;A single punch can crack crab shells and even aquarium glass.;;Its eyes have up to 16 types of photoreceptors and can see polarized light.;;Its club is made of a layered material so tough it has inspired new body armor designs.
Leafy seadragon|Phycodurus eques|Leafy seadragon|Coastal waters of southern and western Australia|Its body is covered in leaf-shaped appendages that make it look like drifting seaweed.;;Like seahorses, the male carries the eggs, attached to the underside of his tail.;;It moves using tiny, almost transparent fins on its neck and back.;;It is the marine emblem of the state of South Australia.
Hoatzin|Opisthocomus hoazin|Hoatzin|Swamps and riverbanks of the Amazon and Orinoco basins|Chicks are born with two claws on each wing, which they use to climb branches.;;It digests leaves by fermentation in a large crop, giving it a manure-like smell and the nickname 'stinkbird'.;;Chicks escape predators by dropping into the water and swimming, then climbing back up.;;It is the national bird of Guyana.
Tufted deer|Elaphodus cephalophus|Tufted deer|Mountain forests of China and Myanmar|Males have tiny horns that are often hidden by a tuft of hair on the forehead.;;Males also have long, fang-like upper canine teeth.;;It barks when alarmed.;;It is mostly active at dawn and dusk.
Pangolin|Manis|Pangolin|Africa and Asia|It is the only mammal covered in large, overlapping keratin scales.;;When threatened, it rolls into a tight armored ball.;;Its sticky tongue can be longer than its body and is anchored near its pelvis.;;Pangolins are thought to be among the most trafficked mammals in the world.
Sea pig|Scotoplanes|Scotoplanes|Deep ocean floors worldwide|It is actually a type of sea cucumber, not a pig.;;It walks across the seabed on tube feet that are inflated with water.;;It feeds by sifting nutrients from mud on the ocean floor.;;Sea pigs can gather in huge herds of hundreds of individuals.
Irrawaddy dolphin|Orcaella brevirostris|Irrawaddy dolphin|Coasts and rivers of South and Southeast Asia|It has a rounded, blunt head with no beak, giving it a smiling look.;;It sometimes spits jets of water from its mouth, possibly to herd fish.;;In Myanmar, some groups traditionally cooperated with fishermen to drive fish into nets.;;Several freshwater populations are critically endangered.
Maned wolf|Chrysocyon brachyurus|Maned wolf|Grasslands of central South America|Despite its name and look, it is not a true wolf; it is in its own genus.;;Its very long legs help it see over tall grass.;;Nearly half of its diet is fruit, especially the 'wolf apple' (lobeira).;;Its urine has a strong smell often compared to cannabis.
Raccoon dog|Nyctereutes procyonoides|Common raccoon dog|East Asia, introduced to Europe|It looks like a raccoon but is a member of the dog family.;;It is the only member of the dog family known to go into a hibernation-like winter sleep.;;In Japan it is known as the tanuki, a famous shapeshifting trickster in folklore.;;Unlike most dogs, it rarely barks.
Dhole|Cuon alpinus|Dhole|Forests of South and Southeast Asia|Packs communicate using distinctive whistles, earning it the name 'whistling dog'.;;Packs can take down prey as large as sambar deer and even fend off tigers.;;Unlike most pack animals, dholes let pups eat first at a kill.;;It is endangered, with an estimated few thousand adults left in the wild.
Solenodon|Solenodon paradoxus|Hispaniolan solenodon|Forests of Hispaniola (Haiti and Dominican Republic)|It is one of very few venomous mammals, delivering venom through grooved teeth.;;Its lineage split from other mammals roughly 70 million years ago, around the time of the dinosaurs.;;It survived the asteroid impact era, but now struggles against introduced dogs and cats.;;Its long, flexible snout is attached to the skull by a ball-and-socket joint.
Bush dog|Speothos venaticus|Bush dog|Forests and wetlands of Central and South America|It has short legs and a stocky body and looks a bit like a cross between a dog and an otter.;;It is an excellent swimmer and has partially webbed feet.;;Packs hunt prey much larger than themselves, such as pacas and capybaras.;;It is rarely seen in the wild and was first described from fossils before living animals were known to science.
